What Maryland Property Records Actually Contain (and How to Read Them)

Open a Maryland property record for the first time and you will face a wall of codes, abbreviations, and numbers: account identifiers, map and grid references, land use codes, liber and folio citations, and several different values for the same parcel. It looks intimidating, but every field has a plain meaning, and once you learn the vocabulary, the record reads like a biography of the property. Maryland Real Estate Records follow the same statewide format, so the skills in this guide work anywhere in Maryland.

Property records exist to answer three basic questions: who owns this land, what is on it, and what is it worth for tax purposes. Every field on the record serves one of those questions. This guide walks through the fields in the order you will meet them, explains what each one means, and notes what the records cannot tell you.

Identifying the Parcel

Every property in Maryland carries a property account number, a unique identifier assigned by SDAT. It ties together the assessment record, the tax bill, and the county's records for that parcel. Alongside it, the record shows map, grid, and parcel identifiers that place the lot on the county tax maps. These codes are the most reliable way to refer to a property, because street addresses can change and owner names certainly do.

The legal description is the formal definition of the parcel's boundaries, often referring to a lot number in a recorded subdivision plat. It is the description that appears in deeds and other legal documents, and it matters far more than the street address when ownership is transferred or a boundary is disputed.

Ownership Information

The record names the current owner of record and shows a mailing address, which is where assessment notices and tax bills are sent. The owner name comes from the most recently recorded deed, so it reflects the last transfer rather than whoever happens to live there. The premises address, the physical location of the property, is listed separately, and the two addresses are often different, for example when a landlord lives elsewhere.

Some records also show the date of the last transfer and a reference to the deed that made it. That reference, a liber and folio, is the key to finding the full document in the land records. A liber is a bound record book, and a folio is a page within it. Together they form a citation, such as a book number and page number, that points to one exact instrument.

Land Characteristics

The land section describes the ground itself. It shows the size of the lot, the land use code, which classifies the property as residential, commercial, agricultural, or another category, and the zoning code, which sets out what the land may legally be used for. Zoning is set by the county, and it controls matters such as building height, setbacks, and whether a business may operate on the site.

Other land fields note whether the property connects to public water and public sewer, and whether it carries a waterfront indicator. In Anne Arundel County, where many homes sit along the Chesapeake Bay and its tributaries, waterfront status affects value, insurance, and the rules that apply to building near the shore.

Building Characteristics

If the parcel has a structure on it, the record describes it in detail. The year built, the total living area in square feet, and a set of codes describing the building type, architectural style, and construction grade are all listed. These characteristics feed directly into the assessed value: a larger, newer, or better-built structure is generally assessed higher than a smaller or older one.

It is worth checking these fields against reality. If an addition was built, a garage converted, or a structure demolished, the record should eventually reflect the change, but updates depend on permits and inspections being reported. An error here can mean an assessment that is too high or too low.

Values and Assessments

The record shows more than one value, and the distinction matters. The appraised land value is the assessed value of the ground alone. The full appraised value adds the buildings and other improvements to the land. The taxable assessment is the figure the tax bill is actually based on, after any credits, exemptions, or assessment caps are applied. These numbers are set for tax purposes on a rolling reassessment cycle, generally once every three years, and they are not the same thing as a market appraisal. To turn an assessment into an actual bill, you need the local rate — see the property tax rates for each Maryland county.

The record also shows the last date the property was assessed. That date tells you how current the numbers are. A value from the most recent cycle is fresh; a value several years old may not reflect recent changes in the neighborhood.

Deeds, Transfers, and Sale History

Transfer information links the assessment record to the recorded history of the property. Each transfer shows a date, and the deed reference points to the recorded instrument on MDLandRec. Reading the deed itself tells you the parties to the transfer, the price where stated, and any conditions or reservations attached to the sale. Following the chain of deeds backward is how title researchers trace ownership through the years.

Sale history is useful beyond title work. It shows how often a property has changed hands and how its price has moved, which helps buyers and owners judge the market. Keep in mind that not every transfer is an open-market sale; transfers between family members or into trusts can appear in the same list at prices that do not reflect market value.

Plats and Maps

A plat is a surveyed drawing of a parcel or subdivision, showing boundaries, lot lines, easements, and rights of way. Plats are filed separately from deeds and are found on Plats.net. The parcel card often carries a plat reference, and the drawing itself is the authoritative picture of what a lot actually includes. For boundary questions, the plat matters more than any written description.

What the Records Do Not Show

Property records are powerful, but they have limits. They do not describe the interior condition of a home, unrecorded agreements between neighbors, or work done without permits. They do not show mortgages in full detail, only the recorded instruments that evidence them. And they reflect the record at a point in time, so a very recent sale or assessment change may not have appeared yet. For legal conclusions about title, a professional title search or an attorney's opinion is still the right step.

Conclusion

A Maryland property record is a structured summary of a parcel's identity, ownership, land, buildings, and value. Learn the handful of codes and citations it uses, and you can read any parcel card in the state with confidence. Start with the account number and legal description, check the ownership and deed reference, review the land and building details, and finish with the values and sale history. That order turns a confusing page of data into a clear picture of a property.
